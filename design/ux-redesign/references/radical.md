# Radical redesign

Use this for a new page, when the developer asks for a radical change, or when the evidence shows the current design can't be fixed in steps.

- Start from the jobs people come to do, not from the current layout.
- Give the evidence for why incremental changes won't get there.
- Name the organising idea in a sentence or two, for example "separate checking the defaults from looking after the list".
- Plan the transition for current users: warn them beforehand, explain what changed and why, and give them a way to send feedback. Compare satisfaction before and after where you can measure it.
- For the most radical changes, consider whether you could let people switch back to the old design for a while. It often isn't practical, so only propose it when the change is large and the audience needs it. Say why it's worth the cost in this case, and treat it as a decision for this task.
- Where possible, propose a staged route: what could ship first without breaking what people know.

For a new page, the transition section usually doesn't apply. Review the pages around it instead, as the plan template describes.

In the plan, fill in the organising idea, the screens, and "Transition for current users". Leave out the ranked list of incremental changes.

Why: a radical redesign pays off mainly when there are few current users or the design has lost its coherence ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/fresh-vs-familiar-aggressive-redesign/)). Experienced users react badly to sudden interface changes. Warning them, explaining the change and listening to their feedback all reduce that ([Sedley, GV Library](https://library.gv.com/change-aversion-why-users-hate-what-you-launched-and-what-to-do-about-it-2fb94ce65766)).
